\section{Background and model}

\subsection{Structural contracts in this paper}

The term \emph{data contract} is overloaded. In some systems it includes business semantics, quality thresholds, temporal guarantees, ownership, and operational policy. In this paper, a contract is narrower: it is the structural shape expected at a checked sink boundary. That shape includes field names, field order where the chosen policy cares about order, nesting through product types, sequence and map structure, field optionality, nested collection optionality, and whether a contract field has a default value. It does not include domain constraints such as valid ranges, allowed enum values, uniqueness across records, or temporal properties across events.

This choice is deliberate. The artifact is strongest where the compiler and the Spark schema model have a direct structural correspondence. Scala~3 macros can inspect case-class structure at compile time, and Spark represents row schemas using \texttt{StructType}, \texttt{StructField}, \texttt{ArrayType}, and \texttt{MapType}~\cite{scala3_reflection,spark_structtype}. That makes a focused structural contract model practical to derive and compare from both sides of the pipeline.

We therefore use the following working definition:

\begin{quote}
A structural data contract is a typed description of the record shape expected at a checked boundary, together with a comparison policy that defines what counts as compatible producer-to-contract drift.
\end{quote}

This framing leaves room for richer contract systems in future work while keeping the current claims honest.

\subsection{Five questions, and which one a witness answers}
\label{sec:five-questions}

``Is this compatible?'' is five questions that take different inputs and are settled by different evidence, and most of the confusion in this area comes from an answer to one of them being offered as an answer to another. Table~\ref{tab:questions} separates them.

\begin{table*}[t]
  \caption{Five questions a boundary has to answer, the input each one takes, and the evidence that settles it. A compile-time witness answers the first.}
  \Description{A table of five rows. Row one: are two declared shapes related, taking two types or schema artifacts and a policy, settled by a structural calculation that can be staged at compile time. Row two: can a reader consume a writer's values, taking writer schema, reader schema and codec semantics, settled by a resolution argument plus codec tests. Row three: did translation preserve the needed facts, taking a source declaration, a mapping and a target representation, settled by mapping laws, counterexamples and an explicit loss report. Row four: does this batch satisfy its contract, taking actual values, settled by a decoder or runtime validation. Row five: is this deployment compatible with active versions, taking a version graph and rollout state, settled by version-aware CI policy and runtime observability.}
  \label{tab:questions}
  \centering
  \scriptsize
  \begin{tabular}{p{0.26\textwidth}p{0.27\textwidth}p{0.33\textwidth}}
    \toprule
    Question & Input & Evidence that settles it \\
    \midrule
    Are two declared shapes related? & Two types or schema artifacts, and a policy & A structural calculation; can be staged at compile time \\
    Can a reader consume a writer's values? & Writer schema, reader schema, codec semantics & A resolution or subtyping argument, plus codec tests \\
    Did a translation preserve the needed facts? & Source declaration, mapping, target representation & Mapping laws, counterexamples, an explicit loss report \\
    Does this batch satisfy its contract? & The actual values & A decoder, or runtime validation \\

The artifact's compile-time witness answers the first question, and only for declarations the build can see. It participates in the others without replacing them: the macro's verdict is an input to a resolution argument, not a substitute for one, and it says nothing at all about values. The row-level check of \S\ref{sec:values} answers the fourth, on the data it is given. Questions three and five are outside the artifact, and \S\ref{sec:lemma} is a result about question three for one particular mapping. Reading this paper as an answer to more than the first and fourth questions is the misreading most available, so each claim below is stated against the question it settles.

\subsection{Compatibility direction, and where structural equality sits}
\label{sec:direction}

The second question needs its direction fixed, because the two readings of ``compatible'' are not symmetric and the vocabulary in circulation does not distinguish them. Write $\mathit{Values}(S)$ for the records schema $S$ admits. For a fragment that decodes by identity, safe producer-to-consumer consumption is containment:
\begin{quote}
$\mathit{Values}(\mathit{producer}) \subseteq \mathit{Values}(\mathit{consumer})$
\end{quote}
Every record the producer can emit is one the consumer admits. A real wire format needs the decoder in the statement rather than the admitted sets alone:
\begin{quote}
for every payload emitted under the writer schema, the reader's decoder accepts it and yields a value satisfying the consumer's schema.
\end{quote}
Defaults, aliases, numeric promotion and unknown-field handling belong to that second relation and not to the first: they are the decoder's behaviour, and two schemas whose admitted sets are unrelated can still be connected by a decoder that supplies a default. Structural equality is then one policy inside this space and not the definition of it. It is the strictest useful point, it is decidable from the declarations alone, and it is what a boundary wants when the two sides are supposed to be the same shape rather than merely connectable. \S\ref{sec:carriers} states which slots the policies compare; \S\ref{sec:evaluation} measures where Spark's predicates sit relative to the containment above, and the containment is what makes the optionality direction derived rather than stipulated.

One further distinction is not in this artifact and is worth naming, because the extension to API contracts in \S\ref{sec:beyond-spark} needs it. A JSON or XML boundary generally has to separate three states of a key -- not supplied, supplied with no value, and supplied with a value -- where a Spark row has two. Scala's \texttt{Option[A]} has two inhabitants and cannot carry all three without a stated codec policy saying which collapse it performs. Separately from the payload, a property can be declared, observed, conservatively inferred, validated, or simply unknown; those are categories of evidence about a schema and not extra values in it, and \S\ref{sec:lemma} is precisely about two of them being indistinguishable after one projection. The present artifact works in the two-state setting, deliberately, and an honest extension to three states is a change to the model and not a new back end for it.

\subsection{The three carriers of optionality}
\label{sec:carriers}

The property this paper characterises needs its slots named precisely, so we state the grammar of a schema that can reach a Spark sink. Writing \texttt{Leaf} for any atomic type:

\begin{quote}
\begin{tabbing}
\texttt{Schema} \= \texttt{::=} \= \texttt{Struct} \kill
\texttt{Schema} \> \texttt{::=} \> \texttt{Struct} \\
\texttt{Struct} \> \texttt{::=} \> \texttt{Field}$^{*}$, an ordered list \\
\texttt{Field} \> \texttt{::=} \> (\texttt{name}, \texttt{dataType}, \texttt{nullable}) \\
\texttt{DataType} \> \texttt{::=} \> \texttt{Leaf} $\mid$ \texttt{Struct} \\
 \> $\mid$ \> \texttt{Array}(\texttt{elementType}, \texttt{containsNull}) \\
 \> $\mid$ \> \texttt{Map}(\texttt{keyType}, \texttt{valueType}, \texttt{valueContainsNull})
\end{tabbing}
\end{quote}

Exactly three of the nine slots in that grammar are booleans asserting that a value may be absent: \code{Field.nullable}, \code{Array.containsNull}, and \code{Map.valueContainsNull}. We call these the \emph{carriers of optionality}. There is no fourth carrier: a \texttt{Map} has no key-optionality slot, because Spark map keys cannot be null. The set is therefore closed by the grammar rather than selected by us, which is what allows the claims in \S\ref{sec:evaluation} to be stated over all carriers rather than over a sample of them.

Two constructs are deliberately outside the grammar above. A \texttt{UserDefinedType} is replaced by its \texttt{sqlType}, and \texttt{CharType} and \texttt{VarcharType} are replaced by \texttt{StringType}, before a schema reaches a sink. An edit to either would measure Spark's analyzer rather than its comparators.

The three carriers are independent in the sense that matters: a schema can require one and relax another, and the number of optionality bits a schema contains does not determine where they sit. \texttt{Option[Seq[String]]} and \texttt{Seq[Option[String]]} carry one optionality bit each in different carriers, and they describe different data.

All three carriers are compared at compile time under every policy except \texttt{Unchecked}, through an explicit \texttt{Optionality} axis with three cases: the producer and contract must agree, the producer may be stricter but never looser, or the carrier is not compared. One axis governs the three, so a policy says the same thing about a field, a sequence element and a map value. The runtime pin compares none of them. \S\ref{sec:weakness} reports what forces that given the information the pin has, \S\ref{sec:lemma} bounds how far the argument reaches, and \S\ref{sec:values} reports the check that replaces the comparison rather than leaving it dropped.

\subsection{Policy family}

Compatibility is not a single relation. Different boundaries need different answers to ``does this producer conform to that contract?'' The artifact makes that choice explicit through a small policy family:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \texttt{Exact} and \texttt{ExactUnordered}: unordered matching by field name, case-sensitive, with no extras and no missing fields.
  \item \texttt{ExactUnorderedCI}: unordered matching by field name, case-insensitive, with no extras and no missing fields.
  \item \texttt{ExactOrdered}: ordered matching by field name, case-sensitive.
  \item \texttt{ExactOrderedCI}: ordered matching by field name, case-insensitive.
  \item \texttt{ExactByPosition}: by-position matching where field names are ignored.
  \item \texttt{Backward}: contract-by-name subset semantics. Producer extras are allowed; missing contract fields are allowed only when the contract field is optional or has a default value.
  \item \texttt{Forward}: producer-by-name subset semantics. Producer fields must all exist in the contract, while the contract may contain additional fields.
  \item \texttt{Unchecked}: compares nothing, so everything conforms. It is named for what it does and not, as in \texttt{v0.1.0}, \texttt{Full}, which reads like Schema Registry's \texttt{FULL} and means the opposite of it: that setting is compatibility in both directions at once, and this one is the absence of a comparison.
\end{itemize}

These policies separate intent from implementation. A sink can declare whether it wants exact matching, position-based matching, or subset-style compatibility with default relaxations. The compile-time and runtime layers then enforce that declared intent at their respective boundaries.
The subset-style policies are intentionally case-sensitive at both layers to avoid ambiguity in subset direction. A case-insensitive variant would be a reasonable extension, but it is not part of the current artifact.

\subsection{Why compile-time proof and runtime pin both exist}
\label{sec:predicate-family}

Compile-time proof answers a question about declared program structure: given producer type \texttt{Out}, contract type \texttt{R}, and policy \texttt{P}, can the compiler prove that \texttt{Out} structurally conforms to \texttt{R} under \texttt{P}? That is a property of code. Runtime pinning answers a different question: does the actual Spark schema reaching the sink still satisfy the declared contract under the same policy? That is a property of runtime data.

The separation matters because data pipelines cross external boundaries. A typed source or transform can be declared against a case class, while the data actually read from files or tables may still drift independently. Compile-time proof cannot see those runtime schemas. Conversely, runtime validation alone comes too late to catch declared drift in the code that wires the pipeline. The artifact therefore treats the two checks as complementary rather than redundant. This split is deliberate: unlike designs that collapse structural validation into a single pass, the artifact separates the checks because they consume different inputs at different times, namely declared Scala types at compile time and external Spark schemas at the sink boundary.

Spark's documented comparator surface helps explain the runtime side. The three relevant comparison entry points are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \texttt{equalsIgnoreCaseAndNullability} for case-insensitive comparison with every carrier of optionality ignored,
  \item \texttt{equalsStructurally} for by-position comparison, and
  \item \texttt{equalsStructurallyByName} for ordered-by-name comparison with a supplied name resolver~\cite{spark_datatype}.
\end{itemize}

All three zip the two field lists positionally, so none of them is an unordered comparison and the first is ordered by name only in the sense that position and name agree. This is read off the bodies at \texttt{v3.5.6} rather than off the method names, which suggest otherwise.


These three documented entry points are not the whole family, so the family has to be delimited by a rule rather than by a list. The rule we state and apply is: every method of \texttt{DataType} or \texttt{StructType} that decides a \texttt{Boolean} about two \texttt{DataType} instances, at any visibility. Applied to \texttt{DataType.scala} and \texttt{StructType.scala} at tag \texttt{v3.5.6} it admits eight named entry points, two of which take a parameter that changes the comparison and are therefore instantiated at both of its values, giving ten configurations. Three methods the rule reaches are excluded for stated reasons: the private recursive worker behind \texttt{equals\allowbreak Ignore\allowbreak Compatible\allowbreak Nullability}, because both of its settings are already measured through the two \texttt{private[sql]} methods that wrap it; \texttt{DataType.acceptsType}, whose body is \texttt{sameType(other)}; and \texttt{StructType.acceptsType} on the companion object, which is \texttt{other.isInstanceOf[StructType]} and answers a question about one type's constructor rather than about two schemas. Visibility is not a criterion: \texttt{DataType.sameType} is \texttt{private[spark]} and the two compatible-nullability methods are \texttt{private[sql]}, all three reached here from a probe file placed in an \texttt{org.apache.spark.sql} subpackage. \S\ref{sec:evaluation} measures all ten. We claim that the rule is stated precisely enough to be re-applied and audited, not that ten is the only defensible delimitation of ``Spark's schema-equality predicates''. None of the ten provides subset semantics, which \texttt{Backward} and \texttt{Forward} need, and none of them lets a caller be strict about one carrier of optionality and lenient about another.

\subsection{Relationship to broader contract work}

The model here is intentionally smaller than the broader software-contract literature. Empirical work shows that contracts matter in real software projects, while more recent work on effectful and trace-oriented contracts shows that the contract design space extends far beyond record shape compatibility~\cite{contracts_in_practice,effectful_software_contracts,trace_contracts}. The right comparison for this paper is therefore not ``have we solved contracts?'' but ``have we built and evidenced a useful compile-time plus runtime mechanism for a focused structural boundary problem?''
