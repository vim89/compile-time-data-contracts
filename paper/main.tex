\begin{abstract}
A Spark \texttt{StructType} records optionality in three independent places: \code{StructField.nullable}, \code{ArrayType.containsNull}, and \code{MapType.valueContainsNull}. Spark's schema-equality predicates relax all three through one \texttt{Boolean}. We measure what that costs. Over a drift taxonomy generated from \texttt{StructType}'s grammar rather than chosen by hand, every schema-equality predicate Spark 3.5.6 exposes is run in both argument orders, and the nine shipped predicates partition into exactly two classes over the three carriers: six accept a change to any of them, three reject a change to any of them, and none discriminates. The hypothesis we set out to confirm, that no predicate can tell \texttt{Option[Seq[String]]} from \texttt{Seq[Option[String]]}, is refuted by the same run and is reported as such. Two specifications written for other systems, Avro's field-matching rule and Confluent's optional-field-based compatibility levels, jointly demand what no shipped predicate provides; of twenty-one predicates measured, four satisfy both, and all four operate on Scala types rather than on schemas. The reason is an information loss upstream of every predicate: \texttt{StructField.nullable} has two values and three producer-side meanings, and Spark's readers collapse two of them, so the claim is checkable where it is still stated and provably not from a \texttt{StructType} alone. In a corpus of 527 Avro schemas from twenty public repositories, 25\,\% are required on one carrier and optional on another, the configuration no shipped predicate can express. A Scala~3 macro artifact occupies the empty cell; its own runtime half is on the failing side of the same line, which is how the line was found.
\end{abstract}
