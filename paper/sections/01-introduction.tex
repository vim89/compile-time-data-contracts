\section{Introduction}

Spark pipelines compare schemas constantly. A sink checks that the frame it is about to write still matches the shape it was built for, a test asserts that a transform produced the expected columns, a framework decides whether a cached plan can be reused. Each of those checks calls one of the schema-equality predicates that \texttt{org.apache.spark.sql.types.DataType} exposes, and each one then reports a single bit: equal, or not~\cite{spark_datatype}.

This paper asks what that bit means. Concretely: \emph{when a Spark predicate reports that two schemas are equal, what has it agreed to?} The question is worth asking because a \texttt{StructType} carries claims that are not all of the same kind. Field names and leaf types are descriptions of data that exists. Optionality is different: it is an assertion about data that does not exist, and Spark records it in three independent places. \texttt{StructField.nullable} says whether a field may be absent, \texttt{ArrayType.containsNull} says whether an array element may be null, and \texttt{MapType.valueContainsNull} says whether a map value may be null~\cite{spark_structtype}. There is no fourth slot, because Spark map keys cannot be null. Three carriers is therefore the complete set, not a selection.

The predicates relax those three carriers through one parameter. \texttt{DataType.equalsStructurally} takes a single \texttt{ignoreNullability: Boolean}, and the other entry points fix that bit rather than exposing it~\cite{spark_source_datatype}. A three-dimensional property is addressed with one bit of vocabulary. That observation is cheap; what it predicts about the shipped predicate family is not, and the prediction is what this paper measures.

\subsection{What was expected, and what was found}
\label{sec:refutation}

The hypothesis under test was that every shipped predicate collapses the three carriers into one, and therefore that none of them can tell \texttt{Option[Seq[String]]} from \texttt{Seq[Option[String]]}: the same number of optionality bits, in different places. Measurement refutes it. \texttt{==}, \texttt{equalsStructurally} with \texttt{ignoreNullability = false}, and both compatible-nullability methods all reject that relocation. Had the table been written from a reading of Spark's source rather than from running it, the refuted sentence would have shipped.

The claim that survives is narrower and sharper. Over a generated taxonomy of structural edits, Spark's ten shipped configurations fall into exactly two groups with respect to the three carriers: six accept a change to any of them, four reject a change to any of them, and none treats the carriers differently from one another. A caller who wants to be strict about array-element optionality and lenient about field nullability -- or the reverse -- cannot express that with any shipped predicate. Boolean blindness does not merely make the API awkward here; it bounds what the whole family can say.

That bound has a consequence that is not a matter of taste, because one boundary needs two things at once that no shipped predicate provides together. The first comes from a specification written for another system: Avro's schema resolution rule states that for records ``the ordering of fields may be different: fields are matched by name,'' so a predicate that rejects a reordered pair reports a false positive against Avro-encoded data~\cite{avro_spec}. The second is a containment fact about one direction of one edit: an optional declaration admits every record a required one admits and one more, so a producer whose field is optional must be rejected against a contract whose field is required, and a predicate that cannot read the field carrier cannot reject it. Confluent's Schema Registry is the evidence that the carrier is one real systems specify over, because its compatibility levels are stated in terms of optional fields~\cite{avro_compatibility}. Of the twenty-two configurations measured here, four satisfy both requirements, and all four are the comparison engine this paper's macro uses, run under rules that read the field carrier.

\subsection{Why a schema-only check cannot close the gap}

The reason is an information loss that happens before any predicate runs. \texttt{StructField.nullable} has two values and has to carry three producer-side meanings: values may be absent, values are always present, and nobody stated a claim. Spark's readers return \texttt{true} for every field of every format that does not record the claim, so the first meaning and the third become indistinguishable once a \texttt{DataFrame} exists. Writing $\pi$ for the map a reader applies to a declaration, two declarations differing on that property can have the same $\pi$, and no function of $\pi$ alone separates them (\S\ref{sec:lemma}). The claim is checkable where it is still stated, in the Scala types where \texttt{Option[A]} and \texttt{A} are different types. The lemma is about functions of the projected schema and not about compile time: a pipeline that carries the declaration beside the frame, or validates rows, has information the lemma does not restrict, and the cost of doing so is not measured here.

This paper's own artifact is on the failing side of that line, which is how the line was found. Turning the field-optionality check on in the runtime comparator broke three builder tests, every candidate rule was then eliminated in turn, and the conclusion was that no rule exists which both fires on real drift and tolerates a CSV read. The compile-time half was fixed; the runtime half is documented as uncheckable and the carrier is dropped through an explicitly named method.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{A characterisation of optionality in Spark's schema comparison.} Over a drift taxonomy generated from \texttt{StructType}'s grammar rather than chosen by hand, the ten schema-equality configurations admitted by a stated discovery rule over Spark 3.5.6's sources (\S\ref{sec:predicate-family}) are measured in both argument orders, and the result is carried to the 4.x line by reading the sources of \texttt{v4.0.4}, \texttt{v4.1.3} and \texttt{v4.2.0}: the predicate bodies are unchanged, the carrier set is still exactly three slots, and the one comparator Spark~4 adds is \texttt{private[sql]} and does not discriminate (\S\ref{sec:spark4}). The ten shipped configurations partition into accept-all and reject-all over the three carriers, with no discriminating configuration; the two compatible-nullability methods are the only direction-sensitive members of the family and are sensitive on exactly the optionality edits, making them nullability subtype checks rather than equality checks; and \texttt{equalsStructurallyByName} constrains the name tree and nothing else, accepting a struct replaced by a scalar of the same name.
  \item \textbf{An externally grounded statement of what is missing, and evidence that it is wanted.} The conjunction of Avro's name-matching rule and a directional containment requirement on the field carrier, which Confluent's optional-field compatibility levels show real systems are specified over, is satisfied by none of Spark's ten configurations and by four of the twenty-two measured. In a corpus of 527 Avro schemas from twenty public repositories, 132 schemas (25\,\%) declare optionality unevenly across the three carriers, which is reported as prevalence of the property and not as a count of schemas no predicate can serve.
  \item \textbf{A mechanism that occupies the empty cell, and the characterisation of why it has to sit where it does.} A Scala~3 macro derivation proves producer-to-contract structural conformance under an explicit policy family, and a sink-boundary runtime pin re-checks the actual frame. The division of labour is not a design preference: the field carrier is checked at compile time because that is the last point at which the claim exists, and the runtime pin deliberately ignores it.
\end{enumerate}

One bound belongs here rather than only in the limitations section, because it determines how every number below should be read. This is a characterisation, not an evaluation of a technique. It reports what Spark's predicates do, what two external specifications demand, and how often real schemas carry the configuration the two disagree about. It does not report that making contract intent explicit improves anything a practitioner would notice: there is no user study, no deployed-pipeline measurement, no time-to-first-drift-catch figure, and no comparison against the cost of a typed-\texttt{Dataset} rewrite. A reader looking for evidence that the mechanism is \emph{worth adopting} will not find it here and should read \S\ref{sec:limits-effectiveness} for what such evidence would have to be.

The empirical methodology is named explicitly at the head of \S\ref{sec:evaluation}, and the weakness the harness found in the artifact itself is reported in \S\ref{sec:weakness} rather than deferred to the limitations section.
