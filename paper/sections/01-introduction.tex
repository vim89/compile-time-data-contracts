\section{Introduction}

Spark pipelines compare schemas constantly. A sink checks that the frame it is about to write still matches the shape it was built for, a test asserts that a transform produced the expected columns, a framework decides whether a cached plan can be reused. Each of those checks calls one of the schema-equality predicates that \texttt{org.apache.spark.sql.types.DataType} exposes, and each one then reports a single bit: equal, or not~\cite{spark_datatype}.

This paper asks what that bit means. Concretely: \emph{when a Spark predicate reports that two schemas are equal, what has it agreed to?} The question is worth asking because a \texttt{StructType} carries claims that are not all of the same kind. Field names and leaf types are descriptions of data that exists. Optionality is different: it is an assertion about data that does not exist, and Spark records it in three independent places. \texttt{StructField.nullable} says whether a field may be absent, \texttt{ArrayType.containsNull} says whether an array element may be null, and \texttt{MapType.valueContainsNull} says whether a map value may be null~\cite{spark_structtype}. There is no fourth slot, because Spark map keys cannot be null. Three carriers is therefore the complete set, not a selection.

The predicates relax those three carriers through one parameter. \texttt{DataType.equalsStructurally} takes a single \texttt{ignoreNullability: Boolean}, and the other entry points fix that bit rather than exposing it~\cite{spark_source_datatype}. A three-dimensional property is addressed with one bit of vocabulary. That observation is cheap; what it predicts about the shipped predicate family is not, and the prediction is what this paper measures.

\subsection{What was expected, and what was found}

The hypothesis under test was that every shipped predicate collapses the three carriers into one, and therefore that none of them can tell \texttt{Option[Seq[String]]} from \texttt{Seq[Option[String]]}: the same number of optionality bits, in different places. Measurement refutes it. \texttt{==}, \texttt{equalsStructurally} with \texttt{ignoreNullability = false}, and \texttt{equalsIgnoreCompatibleNullability} all reject that relocation. Had the table been written from a reading of Spark's source rather than from running it, the refuted sentence would have shipped.

The claim that survives is narrower and sharper. Over a generated taxonomy of structural edits, Spark's nine shipped predicates fall into exactly two groups with respect to the three carriers: six accept a change to any of them, three reject a change to any of them, and none treats the carriers differently from one another. A caller who wants to be strict about array-element optionality and lenient about field nullability -- or the reverse -- cannot express that with any shipped predicate. Boolean blindness does not merely make the API awkward here; it bounds what the whole family can say.

That bound has a consequence that is not a matter of taste, because two specifications written for other systems jointly demand what no shipped predicate provides. Avro's schema resolution rule states that for records ``the ordering of fields may be different: fields are matched by name,'' so a predicate that rejects a reordered pair reports a false positive against Avro-encoded data~\cite{avro_spec}. Confluent's Schema Registry states its compatibility levels in terms of optional fields -- \textsc{backward} permits ``add optional fields, remove fields,'' \textsc{full} permits ``add/remove optional fields only'' -- so a predicate that cannot read field-level optionality cannot decide any of those levels at all~\cite{avro_compatibility}. Of the twenty-one predicates measured here, four satisfy both requirements, and all four are compile-time predicates over Scala types rather than runtime predicates over \texttt{StructType}s.

\subsection{Why the gap cannot be closed at runtime}

The reason is an information loss that happens before any predicate runs. \texttt{StructField.nullable} has two values and has to carry three producer-side meanings: values may be absent, values are always present, and nobody stated a claim. Spark's readers return \texttt{true} for every field of every format that does not record the claim, so the first meaning and the third become indistinguishable once a \texttt{DataFrame} exists. The property is checkable where the claim is still stated -- in the Scala types, where \texttt{Option[A]} and \texttt{A} are different types -- and provably not checkable from a \texttt{StructType} alone.

This paper's own artifact is on the failing side of that line, which is how the line was found. Turning the field-optionality check on in the runtime comparator broke three builder tests, every candidate rule was then eliminated in turn, and the conclusion was that no rule exists which both fires on real drift and tolerates a CSV read. The compile-time half was fixed; the runtime half is documented as uncheckable and the carrier is dropped through an explicitly named method.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{A characterisation of optionality in Spark's schema comparison.} Over a drift taxonomy generated from \texttt{StructType}'s grammar rather than chosen by hand, every schema-equality predicate Spark 3.5.6 exposes is measured in both argument orders. The nine shipped predicates partition into accept-all and reject-all over the three carriers, with no discriminating predicate; \texttt{equalsIgnoreCompatibleNullability} is the only direction-sensitive member of the family and is sensitive on exactly the optionality edits, making it a nullability subtype check rather than an equality check; and \texttt{equalsStructurallyByName} constrains the name tree and nothing else, accepting a struct replaced by a scalar of the same name.
  \item \textbf{An externally grounded statement of what is missing, and evidence that it is wanted.} The conjunction of Avro's name-matching rule and Confluent's optional-field-based compatibility levels is satisfied by none of Spark's nine predicates and by four of the twenty-one measured. In a corpus of 527 Avro schemas from twenty public repositories, 132 schemas (25\,\%) are required on one carrier and optional on another, which is exactly the configuration no shipped predicate can express.
  \item \textbf{A mechanism that occupies the empty cell, and the characterisation of why it has to sit where it does.} A Scala~3 macro derivation proves producer-to-contract structural conformance under an explicit policy family, and a sink-boundary runtime pin re-checks the actual frame. The division of labour is not a design preference: the field carrier is checked at compile time because that is the last point at which the claim exists, and the runtime pin deliberately ignores it.
\end{enumerate}
