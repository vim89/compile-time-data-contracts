\section{Limitations}

\subsection{Limits of the characterisation}

The completeness of the stimuli is of one specific kind and the prose must not be read as claiming more. Every difference between two \texttt{StructType}s is a set of single-slot edits and every single-slot edit is a row of the taxonomy, but \emph{compositions} of edits are not enumerated and no finite enumeration of them exists. One composition family is included, carrier relocation, because it is the one where a predicate can be correct on each slot alone and wrong on the pair. A predicate that is correct on every single edit and wrong on some other composition would not be caught by this design.

Depth generalisation is measured rather than proved. Two depths are instantiated and no predicate diverges between them, which is why a one-depth result is reported as general. That is an empirical uniformity over twenty-one predicates and forty-six edits, not a theorem about recursion, and it would have to be re-measured against a different Spark version.

The predicate population is complete for Spark 3.5.6 and for nothing else. The claims are version-pinned, and a release that adds a comparator or changes an existing one invalidates the counts rather than weakening them. \texttt{DataType.sameType} is \texttt{private[spark]}, so a measurement of it is a measurement of an interface Spark does not promise to keep.

The matrix cannot see what it is not given. It compares \texttt{StructType} to \texttt{StructType}, so for every predicate that consumes only schemas it is structurally blind to the field-level carrier; the field column is meaningful only for the compile-time owner, which consumes types. This is recorded in \S\ref{sec:evaluation} because it was discovered by the harness producing a byte-identical output after a real fix.

\subsection{Limits of the corpus}

The corpus is a convenience sample of open-source systems that read or write Avro, not a random sample of the schemas anyone writes. Twenty repositories were chosen before any schema was looked at and the four candidates dropped are listed with reasons, which makes the selection auditable but does not make it representative. Proprietary schemas, which are the ones the artifact was built for, are absent by construction.

The sample is skewed in two ways that are disclosed rather than corrected. 458 of the 527 parsed schemas sit on test paths, which is why every headline figure is also reported for the 69 source-path schemas; the proportion of interest is higher there, which is the direction that does not flatter a selection effect, but a 69-schema subset is small. And one file holds 33.9\,\% of all field slots in the corpus, so any slot-weighted figure is partly a statement about that file.

Thirty-seven of the 564 manifest entries do not parse, most because they reference a named type defined in a sibling file. A parser shared across a repository would resolve many of them, at the cost of making the result depend on file order. The schemas those files describe are therefore missing from the counts.

Two carriers of the taxonomy have no instance in the corpus for reasons of format rather than of sampling. Avro map keys are always strings, so the map-key rows cannot be instantiated by any Avro corpus; measuring them would require a corpus of schemas from a format with typed map keys. Record depth is also under-reported: the traversal records each named type once across the whole schema rather than once per path, which is what makes it terminate on recursive and heavily reused schemas, and the cost is that the maximum depth figure is a lower bound.

Most importantly, the corpus establishes that the awkward configuration is common in declared schemas. It does not establish that a pipeline reading those schemas would be harmed, nor how often. That would need incident data, which is not available.

\subsection{Limits of the mechanism}

The artifact checks structural compatibility, not business semantics. It can prove that two typed schemas match under a policy; it does not know whether an \texttt{age} must be positive, whether a \texttt{currency} must come from an approved domain, or whether two records must satisfy a temporal or cross-row invariant.

Supported shape breadth is deliberately bounded: product-like case classes, nested products, sequence-like containers, maps with atomic keys, optional fields, nested collection optionality, and defaulted contract fields. That covers the grammar of \S\ref{sec:carriers} but is not a schema system for every Spark or Scala data representation.

Two asymmetries are left in place rather than resolved, and both are recorded because resolving them on symmetry rather than on evidence would be the wrong move. First, the two nested carriers are erased by the file-read path exactly as \texttt{nullable} is, yet the runtime comparator still compares them strictly, and did so before this work. No test exercises a nested carrier arriving from a read, because the test fixtures contain no arrays. This is the same category of problem as the field-carrier defect and should be settled by measurement. Second, the compile-time behaviour change is observable to users: \texttt{v0.1.0} is published, and code that compiled against it can now fail to compile, because the six \texttt{Exact} policies reject field-level optionality drift and \texttt{Backward} and \texttt{Forward} reject the relaxing direction. Runtime pin behaviour is unchanged.

The conclusion that the runtime field carrier is uncheckable is an elimination argument over the candidate rules, not a proof. It is strong because each candidate fails for a different and stated reason, and because the failure is located in Spark's readers rather than in the comparator. A reader who can construct a rule that both fires on real drift and tolerates a CSV read would refute it, and that is the shape a refutation would take.

\subsection{Limits of the overhead measurement}

The benchmark numbers come from two saved snapshots, one local machine and one GitHub-hosted runner. They show that the harness reproduces, not that a stable cross-machine baseline exists. They are micro-benchmarks of schema comparison rather than end-to-end Spark job measurements, and the compile-time percentages are relative to a synthetic project whose baseline compile time is small enough that a fixed cost looks like a large fraction.

\subsection{What is not evidenced at all}

There is no external schema-registry integration, no industrial deployment, and no user study. Nothing here measures whether making contract intent explicit improves anything a practitioner would notice: not time-to-first-drift-catch in CI, not drift catches per month in a deployed pipeline, not the cost of adopting a boundary check against the cost of a typed-Dataset rewrite. Those are the measurements that would turn the characterisation into an effectiveness claim, and none of them is attempted.

FlowForge, the larger framework effort, informed the motivation and the overclaim analysis but is not evidence for any closed claim here. That separation keeps the claims honest and is also the reason the paper stops short of industrial-effectiveness statements.

Nothing is evidenced outside Spark either, and the framing invites the question. Carriers of optionality are a property of schema languages rather than of Spark: JSON Schema records a field's optionality in an object's \texttt{required} list and an element's or value's optionality separately, inside the type union under \texttt{items} or \texttt{additionalProperties}; XML Schema goes further and splits what Spark conflates, separating omission (\texttt{minOccurs="0"}) from presence-with-no-value (\texttt{nillable="true"}), then applying both again to the item element of a repeated particle. A service that consumes a contract published by an upstream system faces the same comparison problem the matrix measures here. Whether the tools that compare those schemas discriminate between the positions, and whether the same erasure happens in their parsers, is an open question that this paper does not answer and that the method used here would answer in the same way.
