\section{Limitations}

\subsection{Limits of the characterisation}
\label{sec:limitations-characterisation}

The suite is bounded and grammar-guided, and the completeness it supports is relative to a fragment rather than to \texttt{StructType}. Every difference between two \texttt{StructType}s \emph{within the fragment} is a set of single-slot edits and every single-slot edit of the fragment is a row, but \emph{compositions} of edits are not enumerated and no finite enumeration of them exists. One composition family is included, carrier relocation, because it is the one where a predicate can be correct on each slot alone and wrong on the pair. A predicate that is correct on every single edit and wrong on some other composition would not be caught by this design.

What the fragment leaves out is listed in \S\ref{sec:evaluation} and printed by the harness, and two of the omissions are worth repeating because a reader could reasonably want a verdict on them. \texttt{StructField.metadata} is a slot a schema really carries and no row edits it. Leaf types are instantiated without their parameters, so a \texttt{DecimalType}'s precision and scale, a timestamp's zone handling and an interval's units are never the thing that differs between a pair. The empty record, duplicate and case-colliding field names, and embeddings of a struct other than the uniform nested wrapper are absent for the same reason. Each of those is a measurement this suite does not contain rather than a measurement it reports as negative.

Within the fragment, most edits are one representative of an equivalence class: \texttt{Int} to \texttt{Long} for widening, \texttt{Int} to \texttt{String} for an unrelated leaf swap, struct to scalar for a change of kind. A predicate that separated two members of one of those classes would not be detected, and that is not hypothetical. Two classes were split only after a verdict was found to turn on which member a stimulus happened to be: a homogeneous field permutation, which three Spark configurations accept and the heterogeneous one does not reveal, and an arity-preserving field replacement. Both are now rows. The honest reading is that the representatives standing for the remaining classes have not been tested the same way.

Depth generalisation is observed, not proved, and the two are kept apart. Two depths are instantiated and no predicate diverges between them. That is an empirical uniformity over twenty-three predicate configurations and fifty-eight rows at two points of an unbounded axis, not a theorem about recursion; the step from it to ``a verdict measured at one depth holds at any depth'' is an extrapolation this paper makes explicit rather than a result it reports. The uniformity does hold at a second Spark version, since the \texttt{4.2.0} run of \S\ref{sec:spark4} reproduces it cell for cell, but that widens the observation rather than converting it into a proof about depth. A predicate whose recursion changed behaviour only below the second level would satisfy every row here.

The predicate population is measured for Spark 3.5.6 and for Spark 4.2.0, and those are two releases rather than two lines. \S\ref{sec:spark4} reports that the predicate bodies are the same text at \texttt{v4.2.0}, that the carrier set is still exactly three slots, that the one comparator Spark~4 adds, in two configurations, is \texttt{private[sql]} and joins the reject-all group, and that the whole matrix re-run against a \texttt{4.2.0} build agrees with the 3.5.6 run in all 2668 cells. What that leaves open is the rest of the line: \texttt{v4.0.4} and \texttt{v4.1.3} are read and not executed, so for those two the carry-forward is still an argument from source identity, and a future release that changes a predicate body invalidates the counts rather than weakening them. The two collation configurations are measured at neither version, for the reason \S\ref{sec:spark4} states: the fragment holds leaf type parameters fixed, so a run would report them as indistinguishable from \texttt{==} by construction. Three of the measured entry points are not public API: \texttt{DataType.sameType} is \texttt{private[spark]}, and the two compatible-nullability methods are \texttt{private[sql]}. A measurement of those three is a measurement of an interface Spark does not promise to keep. A rule that excluded them would have been easier to defend and would also have hidden the family's only directional members, which is why visibility is not a criterion of the rule in \S\ref{sec:predicate-family}.

The matrix measures predicates, not the boundaries they are applied at. Every one of its cells is a pure comparison of two \texttt{StructType}s, evaluated in one JVM, including the twelve configurations this artifact contributes. No cell is produced by invoking a compiler, so no count in \S\ref{sec:evaluation} is evidence that a non-conforming producer type is rejected at compile time; that claim rests on the \texttt{typeCheckErrors} fixtures named in \S\ref{sec:staging}. The matrix's own limit is narrower and worth stating in its place: it shows that the comparison engine the macro uses can satisfy both external requirements, and it cannot show that only a compiler can, because the rows that satisfy both were computed from schemas. The argument for the staging is about input provenance, made in \S\ref{sec:weakness}, not about the predicate.

Nor does the matrix isolate the benefit of staging, and the row family that would come closest does not. The six sink-pin rows are an ordinary local runtime checker applying the same policy vocabulary as the macro, so the pairing of those six against the six unprojected engine rows isolates the cost of one projection; it does not isolate compile time from runtime, because both families run in the same JVM on the same \texttt{StructType} pairs. Isolating staging would need the same policy decided twice over the same producer, once from a declared Scala type and once from the frame that type's data arrives in, with the disagreements counted. The \texttt{typeCheckErrors} fixtures establish that the compile-time arm rejects what it claims to reject, and \S\ref{sec:weakness} establishes that the information the pin needs is gone by the time it runs, but neither is that experiment and this paper does not contain it.

\subsection{Limits of the corpus}
\label{sec:limits-corpus}

The corpus is a convenience sample of open-source systems that read or write Avro, not a random sample of the schemas anyone writes. Twenty repositories were chosen before any schema was looked at and the four candidates dropped are listed with reasons, which makes the selection auditable but does not make it representative. Proprietary schemas, which are the ones the artifact was built for, are absent by construction.

The sample is skewed in two ways that are disclosed rather than corrected. 459 of the 528 parsed schemas sit on test paths, which is why every headline figure is also reported for the 69 source-path schemas; the proportion of interest is higher there, which is the direction that does not flatter a selection effect, but a 69-schema subset is small. And one file holds 33.9\,\% of all field slots in the corpus, so any slot-weighted figure is partly a statement about that file.

The leave-one-repository-out range in \S\ref{sec:evaluation} bounds what those skews can do to the headline and bounds nothing else. It shows that no one repository supplies the result, which is the weakest of the sampling objections. It cannot show that twenty repositories chosen by one person for reading or writing Avro are not collectively unrepresentative of the schemas that matter, and no resampling of the same corpus could. The honest statement is that the headline is robust to the corpus's composition and unvalidated against the population it would need to generalise to.

Thirty-seven of the 565 manifest entries do not parse, most because they reference a named type defined in a sibling file. A parser shared across a repository would resolve many of them, at the cost of making the result depend on file order. The schemas those files describe are therefore missing from the counts.

Two carriers of the taxonomy have no instance in the corpus for reasons of format rather than of sampling. Avro map keys are always strings, so the map-key rows cannot be instantiated by any Avro corpus; measuring them would require a corpus of schemas from a format with typed map keys. Record depth is also under-reported: the traversal records each named type once across the whole schema rather than once per path, which is what makes it terminate on recursive and heavily reused schemas, and the cost is that the maximum depth figure is a lower bound.

The corpus measures Avro declarations and not projected \texttt{StructType}s, and the gap between the two is quantified in \S\ref{sec:evaluation} rather than asserted to be small. It is not small everywhere: 75 of 528 schemas are touched by one of the four divergences against \texttt{SchemaConverters} at \texttt{v3.5.6}, 42 of them among the 132 the headline counts, and the headline falls from 25.0\,\% to 19.9\,\% when restricted to the schemas where the two algorithms agree slot for slot. Both figures are published. What this suite does not contain is a run of the version-pinned converter over the corpus, recording which schemas it refuses and which field slots it introduces. That measurement would answer a different question -- what a reader does to a declaration -- and it is the natural next one, because 18 of these schemas have no \texttt{StructType} at all: the converter throws on a recursive record, so no predicate in this paper's matrix can be applied to them through a Spark read.

Most importantly, the corpus establishes a prevalence and is read as one. 25\,\% of the schemas declare optionality unevenly across the carriers, which makes the carriers' independence a property producers use. It does not establish that a shipped predicate fails on those schemas -- a uniformly strict one does not -- nor that a pipeline reading them would be harmed, nor how often. The first of those is a question the comparator matrix answers and the requirement table reports; the other two would need incident data, which is not available.

\subsection{Limits of the mechanism}

The artifact checks structural compatibility, not business semantics. It can prove that two typed schemas match under a policy; it does not know whether an \texttt{age} must be positive, whether a \texttt{currency} must come from an approved domain, or whether two records must satisfy a temporal or cross-row invariant.

The compile-time guarantee is conditional on the evidence being derived, and the condition is part of the claim rather than an oversight. \texttt{SchemaConforms} is a trait with no members, so an instance can be written by hand, and one that was asserts conformance the macro never checked; the trait is left open because a user's own sink wrapper has to be able to pass the evidence through. What holds is that a pipeline whose evidence came from the macro, and that reaches a sink through the checked API, does not compile when the shapes disagree. A hand-written instance, or a sink reached by some other route, is a decision to skip the check and is visible where it is made. Separately, the compile-time check accepts a leaf type it cannot look inside and compares it by its rendered name, because \texttt{ctdc-core} is Spark-free and whether such a leaf can be written is decided by the writer a sink is given; two leaves with the same name are therefore taken to be the same leaf, which catches a type that changes and is not evidence that the two encode identically.

Supported shape breadth is deliberately bounded: product-like case classes, nested products, sequence-like containers, maps with atomic keys, optional fields, nested collection optionality, and defaulted contract fields. That covers the grammar of \S\ref{sec:carriers} but is not a schema system for every Spark or Scala data representation.

The behaviour change is observable to users in both directions, and \texttt{v0.1.0} was already published, so the revision carries a new version, \texttt{v0.2.0}, rather than re-publishing the old one. At compile time, code that compiled against \texttt{v0.1.0} can now fail to compile, because the six \texttt{Exact} policies reject optionality drift in any of the three carriers and \texttt{Backward} and \texttt{Forward} reject the relaxing direction. At runtime, a pin that previously rejected a frame whose arrays or map values arrived relaxed from a reader now accepts it, which is the defect of \S\ref{sec:weakness} and not a relaxation of intent; callers who want that check back get a stronger one from \texttt{assertNoForbiddenNulls}, at the cost of a pass over the data and with the different meaning \S\ref{sec:values} states.

Two claims about the runtime carriers are made at different strengths and should not be read as one. The lemma of \S\ref{sec:lemma} is a proof about functions of the projected schema alone; it leaves untouched every runtime design that retains the declaration, carries provenance, or inspects rows. Only the last is implemented here, and its cost is not measured against the other two. The claim that this artifact's sink pin cannot check the carriers is the weaker one: an elimination over three candidate rules, each failing for a stated reason. A reader who constructs a total rule that both fires on real drift and tolerates a CSV read would refute it, and that is the shape a refutation would take. The design that argument does not reach, a check permitted to answer ``insufficient evidence'' rather than a Boolean, is left unbuilt rather than ruled out.

\subsection{Limits of the overhead measurement}

The benchmark numbers come from three saved snapshots on two machines, and only one of them measures the submitted head. The local snapshot was re-taken after the engine and comparator rewrites and shows the mechanism became roughly twice as expensive at compile time; the GitHub-hosted runner was not, so its column describes the older commit and the cross-environment comparison is between two runs at that commit rather than between the two machines at the head. They show that the harness reproduces, not that a stable cross-machine baseline exists. They are micro-benchmarks of schema comparison rather than end-to-end Spark job measurements, and the compile-time percentages are relative to a synthetic project whose baseline compile time is small enough that a fixed cost looks like a large fraction.

\subsection{What is not evidenced at all}
\label{sec:limits-effectiveness}

There is no external schema-registry integration, no industrial deployment, and no user study. Nothing here measures whether making contract intent explicit improves anything a practitioner would notice: not time-to-first-drift-catch in CI, not drift catches per month in a deployed pipeline, not the cost of adopting a boundary check against the cost of a typed-Dataset rewrite. Those are the measurements that would turn the characterisation into an effectiveness claim, and none of them is attempted.

FlowForge, the larger framework effort described in \S\ref{sec:context}, informed the motivation and the overclaim analysis but is not evidence for any closed claim here. That separation keeps the claims honest and is also the reason the paper stops short of industrial-effectiveness statements.

\label{sec:beyond-spark}
Nothing is evidenced outside Spark either, and the framing invites the question. Carriers of optionality are a property of schema languages rather than of Spark: JSON Schema records a field's optionality in an object's \texttt{required} list and an element's or value's optionality separately, inside the type union under \texttt{items} or \texttt{additionalProperties}; XML Schema goes further and splits what Spark conflates, separating omission (\texttt{minOccurs="0"}) from presence-with-no-value (\texttt{nillable="true"}), then applying both again to the item element of a repeated particle. A service that consumes a contract published by an upstream system faces the same comparison problem the matrix measures here. Whether the tools that compare those schemas discriminate between the positions, and whether the same erasure happens in their parsers, is an open question that this paper does not answer and that the method used here would answer in the same way.
