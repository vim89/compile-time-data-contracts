\section{Related work}

This paper sits at the intersection of software contracts, static-versus-dynamic checking, and typed data-pipeline boundaries. The closest primary sources for the implementation itself are the Scala~3 reflection documentation and the Spark schema-comparator APIs, because the artifact is built directly on those mechanisms~\cite{scala3_reflection,spark_datatype}. These are mechanism sources, not related-work contributions in the research sense.

The closest implementation-level neighbors are typed-Dataset libraries. \texttt{frameless} and \texttt{iskra} provide typed Spark layers that carry schema information through Dataset operations, and they apply across the job rather than at one boundary~\cite{frameless,iskra}. On the property this paper characterises they are the strongest comparison group available, because a typed layer keeps the field carrier in the types for as long as the data stays inside it: an \texttt{Option[A]} column is a different type from an \texttt{A} column throughout. The limit is where the data enters. A typed layer over an external read is typed against whatever the reader produced, and the reader produced \texttt{nullable = true}, so the claim is reconstructed by declaration at that point exactly as it is here. This artifact requires conformance only at the checked boundary and leaves the rest of the job in raw Spark code; that is a difference in where the check sits, and this paper does not measure which costs less to adopt.

Record-to-record derivation libraries solve a different adjacent problem. Chimney, and earlier Scala generic-derivation work built on \texttt{shapeless}, derive field-to-field transformations with rich compile-time diagnostics~\cite{chimney,shapeless}. That macro technique is close in spirit to the compile-time side of this artifact, but the goal is different: Chimney proves transformation existence, whereas this artifact proves structural conformance under an explicit policy family and couples that witness to a Spark sink-boundary pin.

Table-level schema enforcement is also close, but at a different layer. Delta Lake and Apache Iceberg validate writes against stored table metadata and support schema evolution at that boundary~\cite{delta_lake,apache_iceberg}. Those systems are valuable runtime seatbelts, but they operate after job submission and against stored schemas rather than against declared Scala producer and contract types. The artifact here is therefore complementary rather than competing.

Schema registries are the closest state-of-the-art alternative on the exact property characterised here, and they are the one alternative that does discriminate between carriers. Confluent's Schema Registry checks a candidate schema against registered versions under a named compatibility level, and those levels are defined in terms of optional fields: \textsc{backward} permits ``add optional fields, remove fields,'' \textsc{forward} permits ``remove optional fields, add fields,'' \textsc{full} permits ``add/remove optional fields only,'' and \textsc{none} disables the check~\cite{avro_compatibility}. Because the levels are expressed over Avro schemas, where an optional field is a union containing \texttt{null}, all three carriers of \S\ref{sec:carriers} are visible to the checker and are distinct: a required element inside an optional array is a different Avro schema from an optional element inside a required array. The strength is that the vocabulary is right and the rules are specified.

Two things are worth separating here, because conflating them would make the comparison unfair and would also hide the stronger baseline. The \emph{rule} is Avro's, and deciding it needs nothing deployed: \code{SchemaCompatibility.checkReaderWriterCompatibility} is a library call over two schemas, so a reader can run it in CI with no broker, no registry and no network. This paper therefore measures it rather than discussing it. It is the external baseline of \S\ref{sec:baselines}, run over the same fifty-eight stimuli as everything else, and it satisfies both requirements of \S\ref{sec:requirements} -- the only configuration outside this artifact's engine that does. What a \emph{registry} adds on top of the rule is version storage, a named level and governance over who may register what, and those are what make it a service; the network call is a property of that deployment and not of the compatibility question. Two limits of the rule itself are visible in its measured rows rather than asserted: it decides resolvability rather than conformance, so a leaf widening is accepted in one direction, and it is undefined on the six map-key edits, because Avro map keys are always strings.

What remains a genuine difference is where and over what the check runs. The registry's check happens on registration, so it governs the schema a producer declared and not the frame a Spark job assembled, and the artifact here is reached from a \texttt{StructType} at a sink in a pipeline that writes files rather than topics -- the majority of the workloads it was built for. Reaching Avro's rule from that position needs \texttt{SchemaConverters}, whose four divergences from a declaration are quantified in \S\ref{sec:evaluation}, so the check would then be of the converter's output. The policy names \texttt{Backward} and \texttt{Forward} in this artifact deliberately reuse the registry's vocabulary, but the relation is structural over Scala product types and Spark schemas, and is not a value-level compatibility proof for serialized messages or registered schemas~\cite{avro_spec}.

Runtime data-quality checkers sit on the other side of the boundary and are the alternative most often proposed in place of schema checking. Deequ expresses constraints as a unit-test-like suite over a Spark \texttt{DataFrame} and computes them as aggregations, including \texttt{isComplete} and \texttt{hasCompleteness} on a column~\cite{deequ}; Great Expectations offers the equivalent through \code{expect_column_values_to_not_be_null} and related expectations~\cite{great_expectations}. Their strength is decisive and is the reason they are not competitors: they measure the data rather than the declaration, so a null that Spark's read path silently admitted is still caught. On the three carriers, though, their coverage is uneven in a way that follows from their design. Field-level completeness is a first-class, well-supported check. Element-level and map-value-level completeness are not: a constraint on whether any element inside an array column is null requires explosion or a custom predicate, so the two nested carriers are reachable only by writing the traversal that the schema already encodes. And the cost model is the opposite one. These checks are row-scanning aggregations, paid per record, where a schema comparison is paid once per write. The complementarity is therefore genuine rather than diplomatic: the claim about absent data is checkable statically where it is still stated, and the presence of actual nulls is checkable only dynamically.

The broader contract literature provides the right contrast. Empirical work such as \emph{Contracts in Practice} shows that contract disciplines remain relevant in real software, which supports the motivation for making contract intent explicit~\cite{contracts_in_practice}. More expressive systems such as \emph{Effectful Software Contracts} and \emph{Trace Contracts} show how much richer the contract space becomes when effects, histories, or traces are part of the property being checked~\cite{effectful_software_contracts,trace_contracts}. By comparison, this artifact is deliberately narrower: it checks structural compatibility at a typed boundary.

There is also a useful static-versus-dynamic comparison point. \emph{Dynamic Contract Analysis for Parallel Programming Models} treats dynamic contracts as complementary to static analysis rather than as a complete replacement~\cite{dynamic_contract_analysis}. That lesson aligns with this paper's design choice: compile-time proof and runtime checking do different jobs and should be combined at the boundary instead of being presented as competitors.

Finally, work outside the JVM ecosystem helps position the contribution. P3317R0 argues for contracts that can often be resolved at compile time so that runtime cost can be reduced or removed~\cite{compile_time_resolved_contracts}. The artifact here is not a direct analogue of that C++ proposal, but it shares the same directional idea: when part of a contract question is structural and visible to the compiler, deferring everything to runtime is unnecessary.

Across these alternatives the picture is consistent and is what makes the characterisation worth reporting. Schema registries have the right vocabulary but check the wrong artifact at the wrong time for a file-based pipeline, though their underlying rule is a local function of two schemas and is measured here as a baseline. Data-quality checkers check the right artifact but pay per row and reach the nested carriers only by hand. Table-level enforcement compares against stored metadata after submission. Spark's own predicates run in exactly the right place, once per write over two schemas, and cannot distinguish the three carriers at all. The novelty claim of this paper is therefore narrow and specific, and the measured baseline narrows it further rather than being omitted to protect it: not a new theory of contracts, but a measured account of what one widely used comparison surface agrees to. Avro's resolver shows that the discrimination the surface lacks is expressible at the same staging, so the claim is not that only a compiler can express it. What \S\ref{sec:weakness} establishes is the separate and weaker point that the \emph{field} carrier's claim, once a reader has defaulted it, is no longer in the \texttt{StructType} either implementation reads, so recovering that one claim has to happen upstream of the projection whatever predicate is used.
